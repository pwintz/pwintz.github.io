title: Superposition Principle
term: superposition principle
applies_to: linear systems
levels:
  high-school:
    text: |-
      Consider a medium, such as a stretched string or the air in a room, in which [waves](/dictionary/definitions/wave) can travel.
      The medium satisfies the _superposition principle_ if, whenever two waves can each travel through the medium, the sum of the two waves can also travel through the medium, so that when the waves overlap, the displacement at each point and time is the sum of the [displacements](/dictionary/definitions/displacement) of the two waves.
  undergraduate:
    text: |-
      Consider an equation $L u = 0$, such as a [wave](/dictionary/definitions/wave) equation, where $L$ is a [linear map](/dictionary/definitions/linear-map).
      The _superposition principle_ for the equation states that if $u_1$ and $u_2$ are solutions, then every [linear combination](/dictionary/definitions/linear-combination) of $u_1$ and $u_2$ is a solution:

      $$L(a_1 u_1 + a_2 u_2) = a_1 L u_1 + a_2 L u_2 = 0 \quad \text{for all constants } a_1 \text{ and } a_2.$$
properties:
  - >-
    The superposition principle holds for every [linear system](/dictionary/definitions/linear-system), and fails for nonlinear systems.
  - >-
    [Waves](/dictionary/definitions/wave) of large [amplitude](/dictionary/definitions/amplitude), such as breaking ocean waves or very loud sounds, are nonlinear, so the superposition principle holds only approximately for small waves.
  - >-
    [Interference](/dictionary/definitions/interference) is a consequence of the superposition principle.
  - >-
    The superposition principle lets a complicated [wave](/dictionary/definitions/wave) be split into sinusoids, as with the [Fourier transform](/dictionary/definitions/fourier-transform), and each sinusoid be studied on its own.
