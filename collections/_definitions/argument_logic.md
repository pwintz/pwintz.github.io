---
title: Logical Argument
term: argument (logic)
applies_to: statements
synonyms: [argument, inference]
levels:
  high-school:
    text: |-
      An _argument_ is a list of statements, called [_premises_](/dictionary/definitions/premise), together with one more statement, called the [_conclusion_](/dictionary/definitions/conclusion), that the premises are offered as reasons for.
      For example, "All humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore, Socrates is mortal" is an argument with two premises and the conclusion "Socrates is mortal."
  undergraduate:
    context: logic
    text: |-
      An _argument_ is a finite list of [propositions](/dictionary/definitions/proposition) $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n$, called the [premises](/dictionary/definitions/premise), together with a proposition $q$, called the [conclusion](/dictionary/definitions/conclusion).
      The argument is written

      $$p_1, \; p_2, \; \dots, \; p_n \;\therefore\; q.$$
properties:
  - |-
    An argument is [valid](/dictionary/definitions/valid-argument) or invalid depending only on the form of its [premises](/dictionary/definitions/premise) and [conclusion](/dictionary/definitions/conclusion), not on whether the premises are true.
  - |-
    A [sound](/dictionary/definitions/sound-argument) argument is a [valid](/dictionary/definitions/valid-argument) argument whose [premises](/dictionary/definitions/premise) are all true.
---
The symbol $\therefore$ is read "therefore."

In everyday speech, an "argument" is a disagreement, which is a different meaning.
