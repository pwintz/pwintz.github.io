---
title: Premise
term: premise
applies_to: arguments
levels:
  high-school:
    text: |-
      A _premise_ of an [argument](/dictionary/definitions/argument-logic) is one of the statements that the argument starts from and offers as a reason for its [conclusion](/dictionary/definitions/conclusion).
      For example, in the argument "All humans are mortal; Socrates is human; therefore, Socrates is mortal," the premises are "All humans are mortal" and "Socrates is human."
  undergraduate:
    context: logic
    text: |-
      Consider an [argument](/dictionary/definitions/argument-logic)

      $$p_1, \; p_2, \; \dots, \; p_n \;\therefore\; q.$$

      Each of the [propositions](/dictionary/definitions/proposition) $p_1, p_2, \dots, p_n$ is a _premise_ of the argument.
properties:
  - >-
    A premise need not be true.
    Whether an [argument](/dictionary/definitions/argument-logic) is [valid](/dictionary/definitions/valid-argument) depends only on whether the premises, if true, guarantee the [conclusion](/dictionary/definitions/conclusion).
---
A premise is a premise of one [argument](/dictionary/definitions/argument-logic), whereas an [axiom](/dictionary/definitions/axiom) is a starting point of a whole theory; see [premise vs. axiom](/dictionary/distinctions/premise-vs-axiom).
