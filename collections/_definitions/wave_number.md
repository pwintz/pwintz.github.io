---
published: false
title: Wave Number
term: wave number
applies_to: waves
synonyms: ['wavenumber', 'spatial frequency', '$\tilde{\nu}$']
levels:
  elementary:
    text: |-
      The _wave number_ of a [wave](/dictionary/definitions/wave) is the number of peaks of the wave in each unit of distance, such as the number of peaks in each meter.
  high-school:
    text: |-
      Consider a [wave](/dictionary/definitions/wave) with [wavelength](/dictionary/definitions/wavelength) $\lambda$.
      The _wave number_ of the wave is the number of cycles of the wave per unit distance:

      $$\tilde{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda}.$$
properties:
  - |-
    The _angular wave number_ of a [wave](/dictionary/definitions/wave) is the number of radians of [phase](/dictionary/definitions/phase-wave) per unit distance:

    $$k = \frac{2 \pi}{\lambda} = 2 \pi \tilde{\nu}.$$
  - |-
    A sinusoidal [wave](/dictionary/definitions/wave) traveling along a line with angular wave number $k$ and angular [frequency](/dictionary/definitions/frequency-oscillation) $\omega$ is $A \cos(k x - \omega t + \phi)$.
  - |-
    The angular wave number $k$, the angular [frequency](/dictionary/definitions/frequency-oscillation) $\omega$, and the [wave speed](/dictionary/definitions/wave-speed) $c$ of a sinusoidal [wave](/dictionary/definitions/wave) are related by $\omega = c k$.
  - |-
    The function that gives the angular [frequency](/dictionary/definitions/frequency-oscillation) $\omega$ of a sinusoidal [wave](/dictionary/definitions/wave) in terms of its angular wave number $k$ is called the _dispersion relation_ of the medium.
  - |-
    For a [plane wave](/dictionary/definitions/plane-wave) in space, the _wave vector_ $\vect{k}$ points in the direction of travel, and the length of $\vect{k}$ is the angular wave number.
---
Wave number is measured in units of inverse length, such as $\mathrm{m}^{-1}$.
In spectroscopy, wave numbers are usually given in $\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$.

Many physics books use "wave number" to mean the angular wave number $k$, rather than $\tilde{\nu}$.
