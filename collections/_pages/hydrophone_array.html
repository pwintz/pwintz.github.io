A whale swims past a line of hydrophones (underwater microphones) while making a 500 Hz tone: by default, a 0.3 s call every 1.5 s (other patterns are in the settings below). Sound travels through seawater at about 1500 m/s, so each hydrophone hears each call a little after the whale makes it, and the farther away a hydrophone is, the later it hears the call. Because the whale is moving, the pitch also changes: hydrophones that the whale is swimming toward hear the call slightly higher than 500 Hz, and hydrophones that it is swimming away from hear it lower. This is the Doppler effect.

To hear the delay between two hydrophones, choose one for the left speaker and another for the right (headphones help), for example the hydrophones at the two ends of the array.

Received frequency

The frequency each hydrophone hears over time. The black bars on the dashed line are the calls as the whale makes them, at 500 Hz. The thick colored bars are the same calls as each hydrophone receives them: later, because of the travel time, and higher or lower, because of the Doppler effect. Fainter bars are quieter, since the sound spreads out as it travels. The thin curves show what each hydrophone would hear if the whale called continuously: the pitch is highest while the whale is far off and coming toward the hydrophone, crosses 500 Hz as the whale passes, and drops as the whale moves away.

The thinner, fainter bars are echoes off the surface and seafloor: the same call arriving again a little later and quieter, at a slightly smaller pitch shift, since the bounced path points more steeply up or down and less along the whale's track.

The dashed lines follow one call from the whale to each hydrophone. Hover over a bar to see which bars at the other hydrophones came from the same call, and click it to follow that call instead. Click anywhere else to jump to that time. With the continuous tone, the bars merge into unbroken lines, and hovering picks out a single instant of the tone instead of a whole call.

Travel time

How long ago the sound that each hydrophone is hearing was made. The differences between these curves (the time differences of arrival) are what an array uses to work out where a sound came from: when the whale is to the left, hydrophones on the left hear it first, and the order reverses after it passes.

How it's computed

The whale's position at time \(\tau\) is \(\mathbf{p}(\tau)\) and a hydrophone sits at \(\mathbf{r}\). The sound that reaches the hydrophone at time \(t\) left the whale at the time \(\tau\) for which \[ t = \tau + \frac{\lVert \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{p}(\tau) \rVert}{c}, \] where \(c \approx 1500\) m/s is the speed of sound in seawater. This equation is solved for \(\tau\) with Newton's method, and the hydrophone's recording at time \(t\) is the whale's call at time \(\tau\), made quieter by the distance traveled. The delay is \(t - \tau\).

The Doppler shift comes from how quickly \(\tau\) advances relative to \(t\). Differentiating the equation above gives \[ \frac{f_{\text{heard}}}{f_{\text{call}}} = \frac{d\tau}{dt} = \frac{1}{1 - v_{\text{toward}} / c}, \] where \(v_{\text{toward}}\) is the component of the whale's velocity pointing toward the hydrophone at the moment the sound left the whale. The audio you hear is computed the same way, sample by sample, so the delay and pitch shift in it aren't added separately: they come directly from the geometry.

Reflections

Sound also reaches each hydrophone by bouncing off the sea surface or the seafloor. A flat boundary acts like a mirror: sound that bounces off it travels exactly as far as sound going straight to the hydrophone's mirror image on the other side. With the whale at depth \(0\), the surface at depth \(-s\), and the seafloor at depth \(b\), a hydrophone at depth \(z\) has the mirror images \[ z_{\text{surface}} = -2s - z \quad \text{and} \quad z_{\text{seafloor}} = 2b - z, \] so each echo is computed with the same equations as the direct sound, using an image hydrophone in place of the real one. Since the bounced path is longer, the echo arrives later and quieter. The surface reflects nearly all of the sound but flips its sign, and the seafloor reflects about half. When an echo overlaps the direct sound, the two interfere, which you can hear as the call wavering in loudness. Only single bounces are included; real recordings also contain sound that bounced between the surface and seafloor several times.

Realistic speeds

Real whales swim at only about 2–8 m/s, less than 1% of the speed of sound, so the real Doppler shift is at most about 0.5%, or a tenth of a semitone: measurable in a recording, but too small to hear. The default speed here is greatly exaggerated so the effect is easy to see and hear. The whale swims a track five times as long as its distance from the array, so at a realistic speed its pass lasts many minutes (up to half an hour); use the 10× or 60× playback speed to watch it. Choose "Realistic" from the whale presets to compare.

At speeds close to the speed of sound the effect becomes extreme. Approaching at 90% of the speed of sound, the whale nearly keeps up with its own sound, so a whole call arrives squeezed into a tenth of its length at ten times the pitch. Moving away at the same speed, the pitch drops by only about half. At the speed of sound itself the waves in front would pile up into a shock wave, which is why the speed stops at 90%.
